Why Sound Affects Sleep

Even when you're asleep, your brain continues processing sounds around you. A barking dog, traffic outside, a door closing, or a phone notification can interrupt relaxation and make it harder to stay asleep.

Continuous background sounds may help by reducing the contrast between sudden noises and the surrounding environment — a process often called sound masking.

The goal isn't necessarily to make you unconscious. The goal is to create a stable, calming sound environment that supports relaxation.

White Noise: The Classic Sleep Sound

White noise contains all audible frequencies at similar intensity levels and is often described as sounding like:

  • A running fan
  • Air conditioning
  • Radio static
  • Soft rushing air

Many people use white noise for sleep because it helps mask sudden environmental sounds that might otherwise disturb sleep. Studies investigating white noise and sleep have produced mixed results, but some research suggests it may help certain individuals fall asleep more easily or reduce the impact of disruptive sounds.

Best For

  • Noisy neighborhoods
  • Traffic sounds
  • Apartment living
  • Snoring partners
  • Light sleepers

Common white noise soundscapes: fan ambience, air conditioner hum, soft static, white noise generators.

Brown Noise: The Deep Sleep Favorite

Brown noise is often described as deeper and richer than white noise because it emphasizes lower frequencies. People commonly compare it to:

  • Distant thunder
  • Powerful waterfalls
  • Strong ocean waves
  • Deep rainfall

Unlike white noise, brown noise for sleep produces a lower-pitched sound profile that many users find more soothing and relaxing. However, direct scientific evidence specifically supporting brown noise for sleep remains limited compared with white noise research.

Best For

  • Racing thoughts
  • Relaxation before bed
  • Deep ambient backgrounds
  • People who find white noise too sharp

Popular brown noise soundscapes: deep ocean waves, low thunder ambience, heavy rainfall, waterfall environments.

White Noise vs Brown Noise: Which Is Better?

The truth is that neither sound is universally superior. Different people respond to different sound environments.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is white noise or brown noise better for sleep?

Neither is universally better — it depends on your ears and your environment. White noise is often better at masking sudden sounds like traffic or a snoring partner, while brown noise has a deeper, warmer tone that many people find more soothing for winding down. The best approach is to try both and notice which one helps you relax faster.

How long should I play a sleep soundscape?

Many people play a soundscape all night as a consistent background layer, while others only need it for the first 20 to 30 minutes to fall asleep. A looped soundscape avoids abrupt cuts or gaps in the sound, so it can safely run for as long as you need without interrupting your rest.

Can I combine different sleep sounds together?

Yes. Combining layers, such as soft rain with a low brown-noise base or a gentle instrument tone, is one of the most effective ways to build a soundscape that feels personal rather than generic. Custom soundscape tools let you mix and adjust each layer until it matches exactly what helps you fall asleep.
